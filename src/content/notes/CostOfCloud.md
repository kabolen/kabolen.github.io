## Data Centers, Resource Strain, and a Need for Sustainable Policy
Over the past two decades, the “cloud” has become a ubiquitous but somehow nearly invisible aspect of life. It stores our data, powers our communications, and more recently has become the catalyst for a surge of artificial intelligence. However, this immaterial realm is supported by a vast and very physical infrastructure with data centers at the core. These sprawling warehouse-like facilities are the engine of the digital age, but their increased proliferation is creating profound environmental and socio-economic pressures in the communities which host them. As our collective reliance on data hungry services grows, we must look past the abstract notion of the cloud and instead to the tangible impacts of its supporting infrastructure. This essay explores the current state of data centers, revealing them to be sites of conflict over public resources and community wellbeing. Looking at recent studies, we first examine the strain that data centers put on these resources. Secondly, we shift our attention to some complex consequences imposed on host communities, broadly referred to as “energy gentrification”. Finally, we synthesize policy recommendations which aim to ensure our digital future does not come at the expense of local communities’ health or global climate goals.

The most immediate impact of a new data center is its insatiable hunger for public resources. A common conclusion between scholarly sources, including the ones which are referenced here, is that these facilities are massive consumers of electricity and water in most cases. To put this in perspective, a single center has the capability to use as much energy as a small town of around 2,000 homes and hundreds of millions of gallons of water annually for cooling, straining local resources and infrastructure (Nguyen and Green 2025). This demand is accelerating at an unprecedented pace due to the adoption of artificial intelligence, which requires ever more powerful and energy-hungry computer chips (Barringer 2025).

“& the West” documents how the arrival of data centers necessitates the construction of costly new power plants, the hasty upgrading of existing ones, and the creation of new water lines to supply these centers with ample cooling. This is shaping some regions’ landscape and resource planning strategies, with some areas even limiting how many homes can be built because there is concern about the amount of water available to residents and the town as a whole (Barringer 2025). This is not only a consumption problem, but a zero-sum competition between public and private groups for finite resources. As Libertson, Velkova, and Palm (2021) report in their study of Sweden, when a regional electricity grid reaches its capacity a choice must be made between powering a new corporate data center, supporting the expansion of a local business, or continuing to provide electricity to public transport. This current “first-come first-serve” approach to grid access overwhelmingly favors the large corporate actors instead of local businesses, creating a tough environment for these smaller industries to strive in.

This competition over resources leads to a deeply uneven bargain for local communities, a phenomenon that Libertson, Velkova, and Palm (2021) call “energy gentrification”. They argue that the data centers act as a gentrifying force, akin to how wealthy residents can displace lower income populations from a neighborhood, because the immense energy demand of global tech corporations’ new centers can effectively displace local community members from the energy grid. The policy brief by Ngyuen and Green (2025) provides some stark evidence of this displacement’s economic fallout. They argue that the tax breaks commonly used to attract data centers are an ineffective policy because the location of a data center largely depends on local resource availability, rather than monetary incentive. These same subsidies result in a significant loss of public revenue that would otherwise fund schools or emergency services, all for a facility that creates very few permanent high paying jobs. This dynamic is corroborated by Barringer’s (2025) reporting, which quotes a planning commissioner in Santa Clara, California who refers to data centers as the “tapeworms” of the city because they consume resources and land while contributing very little to the local economy. While some distressed rural towns are welcoming this investment, many of these same areas are passing on the cost of infrastructure upgrades to their residents through increased utility rates. This is all while corporations are negotiating lower costs for their own energy prices (Ngyuen and Green 2025).

This largely unchecked expansion of data centers also puts the industry on a collision course with climate and sustainability goals. According to Ngyuen and Green (2025), the large constant power demand is actively undermining the transition to renewable energy. Because power sources like wind and solar are intermittent, they cannot keep up with the near 100% uptime of data centers. The result of this is that utility companies are forced to delay the retirement of coal plants and even build new natural gas facilities to guarantee a reliable grid. This is a glaring contradiction with labeling data centers as “environmentally friendly” just because they use renewable energy as a main power source. It seems the consensus is that the current model of incentivizing this growth is unsustainable and new policies must promptly be created. Some of these proposals include mandating public transparency in energy and water usage and requiring the companies who propose these centers to fund renewable energy infrastructure construction (Ngyuen and Green 2025). On top of this, there is advocacy for regulations which protect other ratepayers from bearing the infrastructure costs and for the adoption of regulatory models, such as the German Energy Efficiency Act, which mandates strict performance standards and energy audits (Ngyuen and Green 2025; Barringer 2025).

Overall, this study paints us a clear picture; the invisible cloud is sustained by a very real and resource hungry infrastructure that creates significant social and economic costs. The current paradigm, which relies on public subsidies to attract data centers, has proven to be a bad deal for many communities. It fuels a form of “energy gentrification” that strains on public resources, shifts financial burdens onto residents, displaces local economic groups, and actively threatens climate goals by prolonging our dependence on fossil fuels. This raises some questions about the responsibility of governments versus tech corporations in managing this growth and about how local communities can make more informed decisions when data center construction is inevitably proposed near them. The challenge going forward is to figure out how we can build a truly sustainable digital infrastructure that balances the benefits of technological advancement with the needs of the communities which surround and rely on them for various reasons. It seems that more people are becoming more aware of the implications these projects have. Hopefully in the future we develop a system where this progress is actively managed, rather than incentivizing companies and allowing them to get away with deflecting responsibilities onto those “beneath” them.
